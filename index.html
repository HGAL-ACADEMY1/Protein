Hello, young scholar! 👋 Welcome to today's class. I am so excited to explore the world of nutrition with you. Today, we are going to talk about a super important nutrient. It’s called PROTEIN. 🥩🥚

Do you know what the word "Protein" actually means? It comes from a Greek word that simply means "to come first." 🥇

Think about that for a second... "To come first." That tells you just how important it is! It is the most indispensable (that means you absolutely cannot do without it) nutrient. It provides your body with special building blocks called amino acids. 🧱

Just like you need Lego blocks to build a castle, your body needs these amino acids for body building and repairing tissues. If you scrape your knee, protein helps fix it! 🩹

Okay, let's now move on to how we group these proteins. 👇

Classification of Protein

We can group proteins under two big headings: Animal Protein and Plant Protein. 🌿🐄

Animal protein sources are often called Complete Proteins. Why? Because they are like a complete set of Lego blocks—they contain all the essential amino acids your body needs.

For adults, there are eight of these essential blocks: Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Threonine, Tryptophan, and Valine. 🤓

But guess what? For infants (babies), there is a 9th essential amino acid called Histidine. They need this one extra block to grow properly! 👶

Now, scientists also classify proteins based on how they dissolve (solubility) and their physical shape. Let's look at the three main classes:

1️⃣ Simple Proteins: These are the pure ones. When we break them down with water (a process called hydrolysis), they only give us amino acids. Examples include the Albumen in egg whites 🥚, Keratin in your hair 💇, and Globin in your blood.

2️⃣ Conjugated Proteins: Think of these as "Protein Plus." They are protein combined with a non-protein friend. For example, Hemoglobin in your blood is Protein + Heme (iron). Casein in milk is Protein + Phosphoric acid. 🥛

3️⃣ Derived Proteins: These are what we get when proteins are partially broken down by heat or digestion. Imagine smashing a big Lego wall into smaller chunks. These chunks are things like peptones and peptides. 🔥

Now that we understand what they are, let’s talk about what they actually DO for you.

Functions of Protein

Why do you need to eat that chicken or bean soup? Here are the chief functions:

  • 💪 It provides amino acids to build new tissues. This is super important for babies to grow tall and strong.
  • 🛠️ It helps maintain body tissue and replace damaged or worn-out tissues in both adults and children.
  • 🛡️ It helps form hormones, enzymes, and antibodies (the soldiers that fight sickness).
  • 🔋 It can even serve as a source of energy if your body runs out of carbohydrates!

Let’s now dive into the journey food takes inside your body! 🎢

Digestion of Protein

Protein digestion starts in your stomach. Imagine long chains of protein arriving there. There is a sleeping enzyme called Pepsinogen. 😴

When this sleeping enzyme touches the Hydrochloric acid in your stomach, it wakes up and turns into Pepsin! Pepsin starts cutting the long protein chains into smaller units. It even digests collagen, the tough stuff in meat! 🥩

But the real work happens next. The food becomes a liquid mush called chyme and moves into the small intestine (specifically the duodenum).

Here, another enzyme called Enterokinase activates a helper called Trypsinogen into Trypsin. Together with friends like Chymotrypsin, they act like scissors ✂️. They breakdown the polypeptide chains into tiny pieces until they become free amino acids that your body can absorb!

Next, we’ll look at how much protein you actually need. ⚖️

Recommended Allowance

For adult men and women (18 to 35 years old), the rule is simple: One gram of protein for every kilogram of body weight.

But for babies? They are growing fast! 🚀 So, their protein allowance needs to increase as they grow up. Adults, on the other hand, should decrease their allowance as they get older.

For babies in the first 4-6 months, we calculate their needs based on breast milk. Even though some nitrogen in breast milk isn't protein (like urea), babies use it so well that we count it all!

Important Note: 🤰 Pregnant women, nursing mothers, teenagers, and people recovering from surgery or stress need EXTRA protein.

At this point, it’s important to consider what happens if you don't get enough. 😟

Protein Deficiency Diseases

If someone doesn't eat enough protein, they can get a disease called Protein Energy Malnutrition (PEM). We used to call it Protein Caloric Malnutrition.

This can show up in different patterns:
🔸 Kwashiorkor (often seen in children with swollen bellies)
🔸 Marasmus (extreme thinness)
🔸 Marasmic-Kwashiorkor (a mix of both)

Another issue is nutritional liver disease. This is often seen in people who drink too much alcohol or children with Kwashiorkor. It causes an enlarged fatty liver and oedema (swelling). 🏥

Now that we understand the risks, let's talk about how to mix your food correctly! 🍲

Supplementary & Complementary Protein

Remember how we said animal proteins (meat, eggs) are First Class? That's because they have all the essential amino acids. Plant proteins are usually Second Class because they are often missing one or two blocks.

Supplementary Protein: This is when you add an animal protein to a plant protein.
Example: Maize + Meat 🌽🥩 or Groundnut + Crayfish 🥜🦐.
The animal protein supplies the missing blocks!

Complementary Protein: This is magic! ✨ This is when you combine two plant proteins to make a complete meal.
Example: Beans and Rice 🍛.

Here is the science: Grains (like rice/maize) are low in Lysine. Legumes (like beans/groundnut) are low in Methionine. When you eat them together, they cover for each other! It's teamwork! 🤝

Food Sources of Protein

So, where can you find protein? Here is your shopping list! 🛒

🐄 Animal Sources: Fish, crayfish, eggs, milk, goat meat, pork, chicken, turkey, crab, and breast milk.

🌱 Plant Sources: Cowpea seeds, soybeans, groundnuts, locust beans, pinto beans, red kidney beans, and lima beans.

🌾 Fair Sources (Grains): Rice, wheat, maize, guinea corn, acha, and millet.
